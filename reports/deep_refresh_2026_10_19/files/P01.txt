Optimization of inter-basin 
water transfer projects: from 
macroeconomic impacts to 
technical hydraulic design
Hamed Hamzekhani1, S. Jamshid Mousavi1 & Mohammad Vesal2
Water scarcity and supply insecurity are the primary barriers to economic growth in arid and 
semi-arid regions. Inter-basin water transfer projects are high-cost solutions that could create 
socio-environmental side effects. This study presents a novel approach to optimally design such 
projects from an investor’s perspective. We employed an energy input–output analysis-based linear 
programming (LP) model to determine the water demands of various economic sectors within an 
optimal development scenario. The results from this LP model, combined with a survey on the 
sectors’ willingness to pay, are then integrated into a mixed-integer non-linear programming (MINLP) 
model formulated to optimally design the long-distance Khersan Dam-to-Kerman Province water 
conveyance project. The project is a 650-km long water transfer line with a cumulative pumping height 
of around 1500 m whose cost is sensitive to its capacity to be optimized. The model optimizes the 
project’s capacity based on the level of investment economically justified by the sectors demanding 
a sustainable water supply. Our findings indicate that approximately 85 percent of the increase in 
the water demand of Kerman’s economic activities (except agriculture, water provision, and service 
sectors) over the project’s lifetime could be met using only 5.2% of its total capacity. Furthermore, the 
financial participation of these economic activities could enhance the project’s net benefit by up to 
6.3%.
Keywords Optimization, Water conveyance, Input–output, Cost–benefit analysis
The uneven spatial and temporal distribution of Earth’s freshwater resources presents a fundamental challenge 
for water resources management. According to the United Nations, approximately 343 million people worldwide 
(5.8 percent of the global population) living in arid and semi-arid regions face water supply insecurity due to 
climate change and global warming 1. Water resources management under drought conditions necessitates a 
transition from standard operating procedures to adaptive strategies that balance anthropogenic demand with 
ecological preservation. Policies to improve water security generally fall into two categories: a) supply-side 
policies, such as dam construction to address temporal unevenness and water transfer projects to address spatial 
unevenness, and b) demand-side policies, such as water markets, economic incentives (e.g., subsidies, taxes, 
and tariffs), and consumer education. The literature consistently prioritizes demand management over supply 
augmentation2–5. Nevertheless, supply-side solutions can become inevitable in cases of severe water scarcity.
Inter-basin water transfer is a supply-side augmentation option designed to alleviate the uneven spatial 
distribution of water resources 6,7. Globally prominent examples of such infrastructural megaprojects include 
China’s South-to-North Water Diversion8, the California State Water Project in the United States 9, and the 
transboundary Lesotho Highlands Water Project in Southern Africa10. In recent years, increasing attention has 
been devoted to integrated water transport and abstraction management strategies aimed at safeguarding critical 
water reservoirs that provide potable water and essential ecological services. In some cases where the water crisis 
is less severe, solutions such as allocation management can provide adaptation to water scarcity conditions. E.g, 
Lake Mead utilizes a tiered system where water delivery reductions are triggered by specific elevation thresholds, 
to manage systemic shortages in the Colorado River Basin 11,12. Conversely, sever water shortage crises might 
require a combination of abstraction controls and large-scale water conveyance projects (WCPs). A prominent 
example is Lake Urmia, which lost over 80% of its surface area due to severe droughts and unsustainable

The high costs and significant socio-environmental consequences of WCPs typically relegate these initiatives 
to one of the last resorts in water resource management hierarchies. Some scholars further contend that the 
legitimacy of such projects lies primarily in their capacity to supply drinking water, while other uses are viewed 
as less justifiable14. The case of Kerman Province in Iran is one such example. Kerman is the largest province 
in Iran, with a total area of 183,285 sq. km, and is located in an arid region with a mean annual precipitation of 
134 mm15. Groundwater resources provide 87 percent of the potable water and 95 percent of the total water supply 
in Kerman16. However, the province’s main aquifers have experienced drastic declines due to over-exploitation, 
with an average water level drop of more than 0.5 m per year over the past two decades. Consequently, water 
supply security for all purposes, especially for drinking water which requires higher quality and reliability, has 
been seriously endangered2.
The water crisis in Kerman has prompted government action, leading to plans for the construction of a costly 
WCP known as the Khersan Dam Conveyance Line (KDCL). The KDCL is designed to transfer water from the 
Karun Basin to Kerman over a distance of 640 km. Although there is a consensus that the KDCL is a practical 
solution to Kerman’s water shortage, stakeholders have yet to agree on the volume of water to be transferred. 
A key dispute revolves around the necessity of supplying water for productive sectors in addition to meeting 
drinking and sanitation needs. Policy-makers are seeking to share the cost of KDCL with the productive sectors, 
but the financing of the KDCL present a dilemma. On one hand, the project involves high fixed costs, which 
implies a declining unit cost of water as the project’s scale increases. On the other hand, the enormous expense of 
water supply via the KDCL could render the expansion of water-intensive industries either practically infeasible 
or economically unjustified. Therefore, determining the optimal scale of the project and the corresponding 
volume of water to be transferred remains a critical question.
Several studies have examined inter-basin WCPs from technical 8, social 17, environmental18, legal 19, and 
economic20 perspectives. Han, et al. 8 developed a Copula-Bayesian coupling framework to examine how 
often and under what circumstances wet versus dry conditions in the source basin encounter matching or 
mismatching hydrological states in the receiving basin. Wei, et al.17 applied game theory to simulate and resolve 
water allocation and nitrogen reduction conflicts in the Middle Route of China’s South-to-North water transfer 
project. Ding, et al.18 quantified the impact of the Y angtze-to-Huaibe Water Diversion Project’s construction on 
land use and the supply–demand balance of Ecosystem Services in the Anhui section. Wang and Li19 Examined 
how China’s institutions have adapted (or failed to adapt) to manage the middle route of the South-to-North 
Transfer, with emphasis on legal frameworks, governance, institutional fragmentation, and policy mandates. 
Cao, et al. 20 developed a multi-scenario optimization model to improve the operation of inter-basin water 
transfer systems by explicitly balancing transfer costs and supply reliability.
Among the studies evaluating WCPs from an economic perspective, many emploied simulation–
optimization approaches to perform cost–benefit analyses (CBA)5,21–24. Draper, et al.25 proposed an economic–
engineering optimization framework for the California water supply system, demonstrating the practicality of 
optimization models with economic objectives for large-scale water resources management. To achieve a more 
comprehensive CBA, researchers have attempted to quantify the costs of environmental and social impacts of 
WCPs by incorporating them into the analysis as additional economic variables 6,20,24. However, the economic 
benefits of WCPs are still often restricted to revenues from water sales to specific, predefined users—typically 
farmers6,23,24,26. E.g, Nasiri-Gheidari, et al. 24 developed a multi-objective optimization model to identify an 
optimal water transfer pattern that minimizes environmental pollution while maximizing economic benefits 
for the initial plan of the KDCL project; yet their analysis assumes agriculture as the sole beneficiary of the 
transferred water.
While a number of studies have applied economic models—such as computable general equilibrium (CGE) 
and energy input–output analysis (EIOA) frameworks—to assess the economic impacts of WCPs on donor 
and recipient regions21,22,27–29, they rarely address the optimal hydraulic design of the conveyance system 30,31. 
simultaneous consideration of both aspects is essential for developing an optimal WCP plan, as the unit cost 
of water transfer depends directly on the project’s scale and capacity, whereas the transfer volume should be 
determined by demand-side factors, including customers’ willingness to pay (WTP) 32. Accordingly, outputs 
from the economic model should inform a conveyance-line optimization model to determine the project’s 
optimal capacity.
This study employs an EIOA model to incorporate the economic structure of the receiving region of the WCP 
and resource consumption scenarios33 into the optimization of the KDCL project design. Using an EIOA-based 
linear programming (LP) model, water demand for different economic sectors is determined under the optimal 
development scenario, i.e., maximizing economic growth in Kerman province. The EIOA model also estimates 
the water intensity of each economic sector, which is combined with survey data from production units to 
calculate their willingness to pay (WTP) for water. These water demand and WTP values are used to construct 
a discrete benefit function for different volumes of transferred water. Recognizing that the volume of water 
transfer influences both the conveyance line design and the unit cost of water provision, this study considered 
mutual effect of them to provide a more comprehensive solution. Therefore, the resulting benefit function is 
incorporated into a MINLP model to determine the cost of water provision and identify the optimal design of 
the WCP .

Methods and materials
Case study
With a total area of 183.2 km2, Kerman Province is the largest province of Iran. Similar to other provinces located 
in the eastern strip of the country, it suffers from severe water shortages. According to the 2016 population 
census, Kerman’s population is 3.2 million and contributes 3.7% to Iran’s GDP (excluding oil), with the industry, 
agriculture, and mining sectors producing 1.76, 1.9, and 0.7 billion USD, respectively 34. Water scarcity is one 
of the main barriers to economic development in Kerman 35. Kerman’s water supply relies on local surface and 
groundwater resources, providing 10.5% and 89.5% of the total water supply, respectively, i.e., 5598 MCM/year16. 
The groundwater level in Kerman has declined by approximately 0.5 m per year over the past two decades. 
Similarly, surface runoff has decreased by more than 35% compared to its long-term average (IWPC, 2020). In 
addition, groundwater quality has been poor and continues to deteriorate; the concentration of heavy metals—
particularly arsenic—in some aquifer sections is ten times higher than the drinking water standard (INWW 
Eng. Co., 2020). These conditions indicate that Kerman’s local water resources will be insufficient to meet even 
the drinking water demand, estimated to increase by 1.6% annually and reach 561 MCM by 2045, neither 
quantitatively nor qualitatively.
Agricultural activities consume about 92% of the total water supply, and agricultural water use has remained 
almost constant over the past decade. Beyond water quality concerns, meeting drinking water demand by 
reducing agricultural withdrawals is highly challenging, largely due to the difficulty of persuading local farmers. 
Consequently, the KDCL project has become a government priority in the region. As depicted in the spatial 
layout of the KDCL project (Fig.  1), the 650-km conveyance line traverses substantial topographical obstacles, 
extending from the Karun Basin through the Zagros Mountain Range to Kerman (Fig.  4). Notwithstanding an 
elevation differential of approximately 100 m between the origin and destination sites, a cumulative pumping 
head of roughly 1,500 m is required to facilitate water transfer along the conveyance path. This configuration 
necessitates a hydraulic design incorporating multiple high-head pumping stations and pressure-reducing valves 
(PRVs). Furthermore, the deployment of pipelines with higher pressure classes is imperative. While the primary 
purpose of the KDCL is to supply drinking water, it could secure water supply for the economic activities, except 
agriculture and services, with an annual water need of only around 55 MCM and a high share of gross domestic 
production (GDP) (63 percent of Kerman’s GDP) 34. Thus, the project has the potential to act as a driver of 
regional economic development. Moreover, supplying water to economic activities could partially finance this 
high-cost project.
Modeling approach
The methodology adopted in this study involves quantifying the costs and benefits of water supply to different 
economic sectors and then solving a combinatorial nonlinear optimization model to determine both the optimal 
volume of water transfer and the project’s design variables, including pipeline dimensions and length, the

pressure and flow velocity. The model’s objective function maximizes the net benefit of the project, defined as the 
difference between the water price in the donor and receiving regions minus the cost of water provision. While 
the unit cost of water provision generally decreases with project scale, the WTP of water users declines as the 
transfer volume increases36. Clearly, the optimal solution is achieved when the marginal cost of water provision 
equals the marginal WTP of consumers.
Figure  2 illustrates the procedure used to derive the optimal plan for the KDCL project. The proposed 
model comprises two separate modules, i.e., cost and benefit estimation modules, which are incorporated 
simultaneously into the optimization model. In the cost estimation module, several variables and parameters, 
including project scale and electricity price, influence the design of the conveyance line and, consequently, the 
unit cost of water provision. These variables and parameters are functionally dependent and interact through 
governing flow equations and design criteria. To capture these relationships, a simulation model was developed 
based on the KDCL project’s initial plan proposed by Iran Water and Power Resources Development Co. 
(IWPC), as the KDCL project client. Using defined technical standards, construction price lists, and design 
equations, simulation model estimate the cost of water provision at different transfer volumes.
The benefit estimation module quantifies the economic gains from water sales by incorporating both the 
economic sectors’ WTP for water and their projected water demand. The projected water demand of each sector 
was estimated in accordance with their expected expansion over the project’s lifetime. An EIOA-based linear 
programming model (Eqs.  7 to 11) was employed to identify the optimal development pattern under water 
scarcity, determining the expansion trajectory of the economic sectors during the project operation period. The 
WTP of the sectors was estimated using a function fitted to data from selected production units, linking their 
water use intensity with WTP . These data were obtained from contract records provided by the Iran National 
Water and Wastewater Engineering Company, where the prices agreed upon in water sales contracts served as 
the basis for calculating WTP . The outcomes of these estimations were used to derive the relationship between 
water supply and the corresponding benefit was derived and incorporated as a discrete function into the 
optimization model presented in the following section. It is important to note that the proposed methodology 
is developed based on the following key assumptions. First, the significant decline in groundwater levels is 
assumed to constrain further abstraction from local sources, implying that any additional water demand must be 
satisfied through the KDCL project. Second, a portion of the drinking water supply is anticipated to be provided 
via seawater desalination.

Using an EIOA-based linear programming model, we estimate the water demand of the economic sectors 
under an optimal economic development scenario, assuming that local water extraction remains limited to its 
current level due to water scarcity in the region. The EIOA model, a well-known extension of the input–output 
analysis (IOA) developed by Leontief37, is among the widely applied techniques in economics 38. Since the IOA 
does not account for the actual consumption rates of resources, e.g., water and electricity, due to government 
subsidies, the EIOA has been formulated to facilitate resource consumption analysis. The early formulation of 
the EIOA model39 suffers from a data provisioning problem, so Bullard and Herendeen 40 proposed a hybrid-

To develop the EIOA model for Kerman’s economy, data were obtained from the Iran Energy Ministry’s 
Energy Balance Sheet Report  (IEM, 2022), Kerman’s Regional Accounting Report , and the national input–
output (IO) tables published by the Statistical Center of Iran (SCI) 42. The SCI provides national IO tables for 
91 economic sectors every ten years, whereas Kerman’s accounting reports cover 72 sectors annually. Both 
datasets follow the International Standard Industrial Classification (ISIC) framework. The difference in sectoral 
coverage arises because certain economic activities are absent in Kerman Province. To ensure consistency and 
computational efficiency, the economic activities in both datasets were aggregated into 14 broader sectors (see 
Table 1). To regionalize the national Input–Output (IO) table, the Cross-Hauling Adjusted Regionalization 
Method (CHARM)43 was employed. The application of this method relied on two foundational assumptions: 
First, it assumes that Kerman Province’s technical coefficients matrix corresponds directly to that of the national 
economy. This premise is considered robust given the aggregation level and the general similarity of production 
technologies across Iran’s provinces44. Second, the method assumes that the degree of cross-hauling at the regional 
level is proportional to the sectoral heterogeneity observed at the national level. This assumption is supported by 
similarities between regional and national macroeconomic indicators; specifically, Kerman’s proportionate share 
of value-added and total output across most economic sectors, with the exception of Agriculture and Oil & Gas. 
Following the construction of the Kerman’s Input–Output table, Energy Balance Sheet Report45 data were uesed 
to calculate monetary values of water and electricity consumption through the implicit price technique. Finally, 
they were converted into physical quantities to establish the EIOA model for Kerman’s economy.
The EIOA model is employed to identify the optimal development scenario for Kerman’s economy. For 
this purpose, an EIOA-based linear programming (LP) model is formulated with the objective function of 
maximizing regional GDP , leveraging the key feature of the IO table that the sum of final demands equals GDP . 
The model’s constraints account for production technology limitations (matrix A), the maximum exploitable 
capacity of resources, and the production capacity of economic activities. Specifically, the output vector ( x) is 
replaced by the production capacity vector ( x*), as shown in Eq.  9, yielding inequality 10, which defines the 
constraint set of the optimization problem.
 (I− A) −1 × f ≤ x∗ (10)
In inequality 10, the production capacity ( x∗) of all economic activities, except the water sector, is assumed to 
increase proportionally with the population growth rate (PGR = 1.6% per year) over the 25-year project lifetime, 
with the first-year production capacity set equal to the observed output. For the water sector, 250 MCM is added 
to the current supply capacity, representing the maximum volume of water that can be transferred through the 
KDCL project. In addition to the constraint set defined in inequality 10, further constraints are required to 
ensure that residential water and electricity demands are met during the project’ s lifetime. Since these demands 
increase proportionally with population growth, Eqs.  13 and 14 are incorporated into the model to satisfy 
residential demand for water and electricity.
Maximizing the objective function of the model (Eq. 11) subject to the defined constraints yields a solution in 
which the water deficit is offset by reductions in the agricultural sector, as other sectors are more efficient water 
users. However, given the social tensions, reducing agricultural water consumption in the short term may not 
be practically possible. Evidence for this is the failure of Iranian Ministry of Energy’s plan to reduce agricultural 
activity by up to 20% by 2040 to conserve groundwater resources 46; after approximately a decade, agricultural 
activity has not decreased, and some aquifers have even experienced increased groundwater withdrawals by 
local farmers. Therefore, Eq.  15 is introduced as an additional constraint to prevent the model from reducing 
agricultural activity below current levels. Equations 11–15 together constitute the final LP model for determining 
Kerman’ s optimal economic development scenario. Considering limitations in production capacities and water 
resources, the model identifies the production levels of economic activities that maximize regional GDP , with the 
final demands of the economic activities (fk (t)) serving as the model’ s decision variables.

Solving the linear optimization problem (Eqs.  11–15) determines the optimal capacity expansion for all 
economic sectors during the project’ s operational years and allows estimation of the corresponding water 
demand ( Demand (k, t)) to reach this expansion based on sectors’ water use intensity. As noted earlier, 
when water supply exceeds the demand of low water-intensive sectors, sectors with higher water use intensity 
but lower willingness to pay (WTP) become the potential customers for the transferred water. For instance, 
economic activities with low water intensity, such as the automobile industry, use water as a minor input and can 
tolerate higher water prices without significantly affecting production costs; however, their overall water demand 
remains small. Any remaining water exceeding the demand of these high-WTP sectors can then be allocated to 
lower-price sectors with higher water intensity, up to the point where the consumers’ WTP be higher than the 
marginal cost of water supply through the KDCL project. This effectively allows water to be offered at different 
prices to different users, resembling a pay-as-bid market for water.
T o estimate the WTP of different economic sectors based on their water use intensity derived from the 
EIO table, we reviewed water sale contracts—specifically, effluent from wastewater treatment plants—between 
several production units and INWW Eng. Co. This review indicated that the lower the contribution of water 
to an economic activity’ s total inputs, the greater the amount the activity is willing to pay for water (Fig.  3). 
Accordingly, we assume that the WTP for water in Kerman’ s economic sectors can be estimated from their 
water use intensity. Based on this assumption, the data obtained in this review were generalized using a curve-
fitting approach to derive a functional relationship between the sectors’ water use intensity and their WTP . 
Several functional forms were tested, and a logarithmic function with the highest R 2 value (0.937) was selected 
(Fig. 3). This function was then used to determine the water tariff for each economic sector, as reported in Table 
2. It should be noted that, to ensure real-world applicability, the estimated WTP values were adjusted for each 
economic sector.
Cost estimation module
Fluid mechanics equations, technical standards, and official project price lists are applied in this module to 
develop a model for estimating the project’s implementation costs (Eqs. 16–21) and operation costs (Eqs.  22–
35). As a sub-module of the CBA optimization framework (Eqs. 1–4), this model determines the optimal values 
of the project’s design variables, aiming to minimize total costs for a given volume of water transfer. While 
design variables such as pipe diameters and pump station characteristics are optimized by the model, other 
parameters—such as the conveyance route layout and pipe material—are assumed to be fixed and predetermined 
according to the initial plan proposed by the project’s client (IWPC). In the preliminary design, steel pipes with 
a maximum working pressure of 40 bar were identified as the best techno-economically option for the KDCL 
project. Since steel pipes are manufactured in standard discrete diameters, pipe diameter cannot be treated as a 
continuous decision variable.
The layout of the project’s conveyance line suggested through the feasibility studies conducted by IWPC 
(Fig. 1) was adopted in this study for the optimal hydraulic design of the KDCL system. Figure  4 shows the 
profile of the conveyance line. Since the highest and lowest elevations along the route represent the critical 
points, the conveyance path is divided into seven main segments corresponding to the major depressions and 
reliefs in the profile. Each segment is divided into four subsegments, allowing each to have a distinct pipe 
diameter—resulting in 28 segmental diameters defined as decision variables. The lengths of the subsegments 
are optimized, with their total length constrained to equal that of the corresponding segment. Pressure reducing 
valves (PRVs) and pumping stations can be installed only at the beginning of each subsegment. Due to the 
presence of binary variables, increasing the number of subsegments significantly raises computational costs 
while having negligible impact on the optimal solution as discussed in the results and discussion section. The 
optimization model determines the optimal values of all the design variables—including pipe diameters, flow 
velocities, and the placement and specifications of pump stations and PRVs—to maximize the objective function 
(net benefit).

In total, the optimization framework (Eqs. 1 to 4 ) handles 662 main decision variables. This includes 196 
binary variables dedicated to pipe diameter selection (168) and PRV installation locations (28), as well as 84 
continuous variables governing hydraulic parameters (such as pipe lengths, pressure reduction levels, and 
pumping power), and finally 350 continuous variables to identify the water allocation pattern across 14 economic 
sectors over the 25-year project lifetime. It should be noted that the model also incorporates numerous auxiliary 
variables, such as flow velocity in the pipes and hydraulic head at the end of sub-segments, whose values are 
strictly dependent on these primary decision variables.
Key fixed parameters within the model, including the Hazen–Williams coefficient, velocity bounds, and 
working pressure limits were established based on Iran’ s standard for designing water supply systems, known 
as Publication 3-117 47 and the steel pipes specifications provided in the main manufacturer’ s catalog 48. The 
above MINLP model was solved using the ANTIGONE solver, a robust global optimization tool available in 
GAMS. ANTIGONE employs the Branch-and-Reduce-and-Cut (BARC) algorithm, which integrates branch-
and-bound, reduced gradient, and cutting-plane methods to efficiently search for the global optimum of non-
convex and nonlinear problems. This approach iteratively partitions the problem into subproblems and refines 
the solution space until the global minimum is identified. The solver is particularly effective for highly nonlinear 
and non-convex formulations where conventional techniques often fail to converge to optimal solutions. T o

Results and discussion
The results of of this study reveal several key quantitative outcomes. Under the optimal solution, 117.7 MCM/
year of water is transferred through the KDCL system, of which 111.5 MCM/year (≈94%) is allocated to 
drinking water supply and 6.2 MCM/year to economic sectors. The estimated marginal cost of transferred water 
is approximately $0.7 per m3, which acts as a threshold determining whether supplying water to specific sectors 
is economically justifiable from the investor’s perspective. Consequently, water supply to agriculture, service, 
and water provision sectors is excluded in the optimal allocation because their willingness to pay does not cover 
this cost. Despite allocating only a small portion of the system capacity to economic activities, the project is able 
to satisfy more than 85% of the incremental water demand of most industrial sectors at reasonable cost. The 
following subsections present the detailed outcomes of the two optimization stages and discuss their economic 
and hydraulic implications.
The proposed framework involves two levels of optimization: first, the outcome of a simple EIOA-based 
LP model used to determine the production capacity expansion in the economic sectors that would maximize 
regional GDP under conditions where further extraction from local water resources is not possible and the 
full capacity (250 MCM/year) of the water transfer project is utilized. Accordingly, the water demands of the 
economic sectors required to support their projected production capacity expansion are calculated. Second, 
the outcome of a more complex MINLP model solved to identify supplying what portion of the water demand 
determined in the first step is economically justifiable from the project investor’s perspective, as well as optimal 
hydraulic design of the KDCL project.
Table 4, derived from solving the EIOA-based LP model (Eqs. 11–15), presents the calculated water demand 
of economic sectors required to achieve the optimal production capacity expansion—approximately 111.5 
MCM/year out of the total 250 MCM/year should be reserved to meet drinking water demand. Table 4 indicates 
that the capacity expansion of all economic sectors almost follows the expected trajectory in line with the PGR, 
except for the agriculture sector, where limited water resources constrain expansion. In fact, the production 
capacities of less water-intensive industries are fully utilized to achieve the highest possible GDP growth under 
water-scarce conditions. It is also notable that as approaching the final years, the economic growth rate gradually 
declines due to increasing water shortages, decreasing from approximately 1.6% in the first year to about 1.2% 
in the final year.
Using the estimated water demands of the economic activities (Table 4) and their willingness to pay for 
water (Table 2), Eqs. 1–4 were solved in conjunction with the benefit and cost estimation modules Eqs. 5–8 and 
16–35 to determine the optimal water allocation among economic sectors and the optimal hydraulic design 
of the project. In the optimal solution, out of the total feasible water transfer capacity of 250 MCM/year, 117.7 
MCM/year is utilized—111.5 MCM/year allocated to drinking water supply and 6.2 MCM/year to economic 
sectors. Although only 5.3% of the conveyance system’s total transfer capacity is assigned to economic activities, 
the project can supply over 85% of the increase in water demand among these sectors at a reasonable cost—
excluding the service, water provision, and agriculture sectors. Table 5 presents the percentages of the water 
demands listed in Table 4 that can be met by the KDCL project at the obtained optimal solution.

Since the primary objective of the project is to address the drinking water deficit in Kerman Province, 
approximately 94% of the total transmission capacity (111.5 MCM) is allocated to meeting the sector’s final 
water demand. Accordingly, the project’s net benefits for private investors largely depend on the tariff charged 
to households for the transferred water. Considering a price of $1/m 3, set slightly above the marginal cost of 
water supply to provide incentives for private sector investment, the project’s net profit was evaluated under two 
different scenarios: (1) supplying water solely for domestic use, and (2) supplying water to both domestic and 
economic sectors. In the first scenario, the present value (PV) of total project costs is estimated at approximately 
$806.1 million, while in the second scenario, it slightly increases to $823.6 million. Nevertheless, due to the 
additional revenue generated from water sales to economic sectors, the PV of the project’s net benefit rising 
from $384.5 million to $408.7 million in the second scenario. The PV of payments made by economic activities 
for water is estimated at $41.6 million, increasing the project’s net benefit by approximately 6.3%. Notably, this 
contribution is calculated using an 8% discount rate, in line with standard practice in Iran. A lower discount 
rate would further increase the contribution of economic activities, as most payments are scheduled for the later 
years of the project.
Water price is a critical determinant for water -intensive economic activities, as it directly affects their 
profitability, and in some sectors, limited access to water at economically viable prices can significantly constrain 
or even halt further growth. Accordingly, ensuring an adequate and reliable water supply for certain sectors—
such as services and electricity—is essential, even if the payments received do not fully cover production costs, 
as these sectors are fundamental to public welfare. Moreover, given the prevailing socioeconomic conditions in 
Kerman, households cannot be charged high tariffs for drinking water. Despite the strong rationale for providing 
subsidized water to these consumer groups, this study assumes that water supplied through the KDCL project is 
allocated only to consumers who are able to cover its cost, subject to the constraint that drinking water demand 
must be fully met. However, policymakers may ultimately choose to subsidize water for specific groups, such 
as domestic users. Another point that should be taken into consideration is that, in this study— The prevailing 
conditions in the non-conventional water market in Iran—water pricing for economic users is assumed to occur 
through contractual arrangements rather than through a standard market-clearing mechanism. This system 
resembles a pay-as-bid structure in which water prices vary across users. Consequently, some sectors pay below 
the equivalent marginal price of water, while others pay above it. This pricing framework enables water to be 
allocated to a larger number of economic activities, thereby contributing to higher regional GDP .

in the event of flow interruption. The energy line indicates that the hydraulic gradient remains within a safe 
range throughout the system, ensuring efficient energy use and stable operation. This configuration minimizes 
both energy consumption and implementation costs while maintaining adequate pressure across all segments 
of the conveyance system. The results also confirm the appropriateness of dividing each main segment into four

The results of the sensitivity analysis on the parameters AFOpr and AFImp are presented in Fig.  7, which 
illustrates how changes in these two parameters affect the project’ s net benefit and the volume of water transferred. 
Increasing these adjustment factors corresponds to higher project costs; consequently, the project’ s net benefit

the project’ s net benefit declines continuously and gradually, the reduction in the transferred water volume is not 
gradual, showing several sudden drops. This indicates that, due to the imposed tariff structure, certain economic

making water supply to those sectors no longer economically justifiable for the investor of the project. Provided 
that the project’ s side costs exceed 65 percent of the main cost (AFOpr and AFImp coefficients are more than

tariffs sat for selling water to economic sectors (Table 2) must be increased to create an incentive for investment 
in this project.
Conclusion
High-cost inter-basin WCPs, despite their potential socio-environmental impacts, are sometimes the only 
reliable solution to severe water scarcity challenges. Ensuring a sustained water supply to Kerman Province—
an arid region where water shortages significantly hinder development—has been a major concern for 
policymakers, leading to the initiation of the KDCL project to meet the province’s drinking water demand in the 
first place. However, the high implementation and operational costs necessitate the involvement of economic 
sectors to help mitigate the financial burden while simultaneously addressing their own water shortage 
problem. This study presents a novel optimization framework for the optimal design of the long-distance KDCL 
project, simultaneously addressing the interrelated challenges of determining the project’s optimal hydraulic 
configuration, water transfer volume, sectoral production capacity expansion, and water allocation among 
economic sectors. In the proposed approach, sectoral production capacities—and consequently, their water 
demands—are first determined using an EIOA-based linear programming model. The resulting values are then 
incorporated into a mixed-integer nonlinear programming (MINLP) model, solved using the BARC algorithm 
to achieve globally optimal design and allocation pattern in this project.
The results indicate that the participation of less water-intensive economic sectors in this large-scale drinking 
water supply project not only satisfies their water demands but also helps offset part of the project’s costs. 
However, supplying water to highly water-intensive sectors—such as agriculture—through such a relatively 
high-cost project is not economically justified. The proposed method effectively determines the optimal water 
allocation among economic sectors, as well as their respective contribution shares to the project’s benefits and 
costs. One of the key advantages of this approach lies in its ability to prescribe an optimal economic development 
strategy for regions facing water scarcity. Future research may focus on extending the economic component of 
the model into a more advanced models, such as a Computable General Equilibrium (CGE) model, or enhancing 
the EIOA model to account for dynamic economic conditions.
